# ep04 — What It Feels Like to Be Struck by Lightning

- **Adapted from (FR)** : sujet natif US (même structure que « Voici ce qu'on ressent en mourant… »)
- **Target length** : ~7 min · ~1 000 words
- **US setting** : fishing boat on a lake in central Florida, July, 3:40 p.m.

## Long-form script

### 1. COLD OPEN
> [SFX: water lapping, a distant rumble]
> You hear thunder. Far away. The sky over the lake is still blue above you.
> You think you have time to make one more cast.
> [beat]
> You don't.

### 2. CONTRACT
> In the next few minutes, you're going to live through a lightning strike — a fraction of a second where your heart stops… and then something stranger happens.
> And by the end, you'll know why most lightning victims who die could have been saved by someone standing right next to them.

### 3. CONTEXT
> [ON SCREEN: Florida = most lightning deaths in the US]
> Florida is the lightning capital of America. Summer afternoons, the heat lifts wet air off the ground, and by three o'clock the storms build like clockwork.
> Your odds of being struck in your lifetime are about one in fifteen thousand. Every year, a few hundred Americans are hit. About one in ten of them dies.
> [beat]
> And most of them were outside doing something ordinary. Fishing. Golfing. Walking to their car.

### 4. TIMELINE

#### [TIMER 00:00.000] — The leader
> High above you, a channel of charged air is zig-zagging down from the cloud, invisible, fifty yards at a time.
> As it gets close, the ground answers. Tiny streamers of charge rise up from tall things — trees, antennas, fishing rods… and the top of your head.
> Your hair might stand up. Your skin might tingle.
> That's the only warning you get.

#### [TIMER 00:00.001] — The strike
> [SFX: CRACK — white flash]
> The two channels connect. Up to three hundred million volts. Tens of thousands of amps. The air in the channel heats to around fifty thousand degrees — hotter than the surface of the sun.
> And yet, here's the surprise: most of that current doesn't go *through* you.

#### [TIMER 00:00.002] — The flashover
> [X-RAY LAYER: skin outline glowing]
> The strike is so fast that most of the energy races over the outside of your body, across your wet skin and sweat. Doctors call it a flashover.
> The sweat flashes into steam. It can blow your shoes off your feet and tear open your clothes.
> That flashover is why most people survive lightning. It kept the worst of it from crossing your chest.
> But not all of it.

#### [TIMER 00:00.003] — Your heart stops
> [SFX: heartbeat — then silence]
> Some of the current does cross your heart. Every heart cell gets the same massive electrical signal at the same instant — like hitting every key on a piano at once.
> Your heart stops. Completely.

#### [TIMER 00:05] — It starts again
> And then — the strangest part. Your heart has its own built-in pacemaker, a small cluster of cells that fires on its own.
> A few seconds later, it fires again. *Thump.* Your heart restarts by itself.
> [beat]
> Your lungs don't.

### 5. POINT OF NO RETURN
> [X-RAY LAYER: brainstem dimming]
> The same current stunned the breathing center in your brainstem. It can stay offline far longer than your heart did.
> So your heart is beating — pumping blood that has less and less oxygen in it.
> After a few minutes without a breath, your heart is starving. It starts to quiver instead of pumping. This time, it won't restart on its own.

### 6. THE LAST THING YOU FEEL
> If you could feel anything, it would be strange. Your ears ring from a ruptured eardrum — the thunder went off right next to your head.
> Your legs may feel heavy, cold and blue, and refuse to move. Doctors call it keraunoparalysis. It's temporary — if you live.
> On your skin, a red pattern blooms like the branches of a fern. It's called a Lichtenberg figure. It will fade in a few days.
> [beat]
> But you don't remember any of it. You were unconscious from the first millisecond.
> [BLACK]

### 7. WHAT SAVES YOU
> [warm light — after the storm]
> Here's what almost nobody knows: a lightning victim does not hold an electrical charge. You can touch them right away.
> And because the heart often restarts by itself but the breathing doesn't, CPR and rescue breaths in those first minutes can bring them back.
> [ON SCREEN: 1. Call 911 · 2. Safe to touch · 3. Not breathing? Start CPR · 4. Use an AED if there is one]
> When several people are hit at once, rescuers do the opposite of normal triage: they help the ones who look dead first. The ones moaning and moving are already breathing.
> And to avoid all of this: if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Lightning can hit ten miles from the storm. Get inside a building or a hard-topped car, and wait thirty minutes after the last thunder.
> [beat]
> When thunder roars, go indoors.

### 8. NEXT
> If you want to know what it feels like to be inside the other great American storm — a tornado — that's the next video.

## Short (≈ 50 s)

> You hear thunder far away. You think you have time for one more cast.
> 0.001 seconds: three hundred million volts.
> Most of it races over your wet skin — the sweat flashes to steam and can blow your shoes off.
> Some crosses your heart. It stops.
> Five seconds later, it restarts on its own.
> But your breathing doesn't. That's what kills you — minutes later.
> So if someone gets hit: they're safe to touch. Start CPR. You could be the reason their heart doesn't stop twice.
> And next time you hear thunder far away… you don't have time for one more cast.
