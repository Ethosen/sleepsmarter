# ep01 — This Is What It Feels Like to Be Hit by a Car

- **Adapted from (FR)** : « Voici ce qu'on ressent en mourant écrasé par une voiture »
- **Target length** : ~7 min · ~1 050 words at 150 wpm
- **US setting** : six-lane arterial road in Phoenix, Arizona, 9:40 p.m., no crosswalk nearby

## Long-form script

### 1. COLD OPEN
> [SFX: traffic hum, a distant car stereo]
> You step off the curb. Six lanes. The nearest crosswalk is a quarter mile away, and you're tired.
> [beat]
> You've done this a hundred times.

### 2. CONTRACT
> Over the next few minutes, you're going to live through the next two seconds of your life —
> one tenth of a second at a time.
> And by the end, you'll know the single number that decides whether you walk away… or don't.

### 3. CONTEXT
> [ON SCREEN: 7,148 pedestrians killed in 2024]
> In 2024, more than seven thousand people were killed walking on American roads.
> That's one every seventy-four minutes.
> Most of them weren't jaywalking across a quiet street. They were on wide, fast roads like this one —
> at night, away from an intersection.
> [beat]
> Like you.

### 4. TIMELINE

#### [TIMER 00:00.0] — The headlights
> The car is doing forty-five. The driver is looking at a text.
> You see the lights — but your brain files them as "far away." Judging the speed of something
> coming straight at you, at night, is one of the things human eyes are worst at.
> By the time you understand, you have less than a second. Your body starts to turn. That's all it has time for.

#### [TIMER 00:00.1] — First contact
> What hits you first depends on what's coming at you.
> If it's a sedan, the bumper meets your legs, just below the knee. Doctors even have a name for the injury it leaves: a *bumper fracture.*
> Your legs are swept out from under you, and your upper body folds onto the hood.
> [beat]
> But this isn't a sedan. It's a full-size pickup.
> [ON SCREEN: hood height 40+ inches → ~45% more likely to kill a pedestrian]
> Its front end is a flat wall at the height of your hips and chest. It doesn't sweep you up. It pushes you forward — and down.

#### [TIMER 00:00.2] — Inside your body
> [X-RAY LAYER]
> Your body has just gone from zero to forty-five miles per hour in a fraction of a second.
> Your skeleton moves with the truck. Your organs don't — not right away. They keep their old speed for an instant, and slam into the inside of your ribs.
> Your heart swings on the large artery holding it in place, the aorta. In violent impacts, that's where it can tear.

#### [TIMER 00:00.5] — No pain
> Here's what almost every survivor says: it didn't hurt. Not yet.
> At the moment of impact, your brain floods your system with its own painkillers — chemicals that work on the same receptors as morphine. Your adrenal glands dump adrenaline. Your heart rate spikes.
> Pain is a signal that says "stop and protect the wound." Right now, your brain has decided it has more urgent things to do.

#### [TIMER 00:01.0] — Time slows down
> You may feel that time stretches. That you watched it happen in slow motion.
> Researchers have tested this — and your brain isn't actually running faster. What changes is memory. Under extreme fear, you record more detail per second. When you remember it later, the moment feels longer than it was.
> [beat]
> That is, if you remember it at all.

#### [TIMER 00:01.5] — The second impact
> Most people think the car is what kills you. Often, it's the ground.
> You hit the asphalt at close to the speed of the truck. Your head, the heaviest part of you for its size, keeps moving after your shoulders stop.
> Inside your skull, your brain — which floats in fluid — strikes the bone. Then rebounds and strikes the opposite side.

### 5. POINT OF NO RETURN
> [SFX: high-pitched ring, everything muffled]
> Brain injury is the most common way people die in these crashes.
> As your brain swells inside a skull that cannot expand, the pressure rises. Blood can no longer get in.
> Somewhere in your pelvis, a broken bone has cut a vessel. You are bleeding where nobody can see it.

### 6. THE LAST THING YOU FEEL
> If you're conscious, you might feel cold. The asphalt is still warm from the Arizona sun, but you're cold, because your body has pulled its blood away from your skin to protect the organs that still matter.
> Sounds turn distant. Someone is shouting. A phone light in your face.
> [beat]
> And most survivors don't remember any of this. A violent blow to the head often wipes out the minutes before and after it. The brain was too busy to write it down.
> [SFX: heartbeat slows… stops] [BLACK]

### 7. WHAT SAVES YOU
> [warm light, morning, same road]
> Remember that single number? It's speed.
> [ON SCREEN: 23 mph → 10% · 32 mph → 25% · 42 mph → 50% · 50 mph → 75% · 58 mph → 90%]
> A pedestrian hit at twenty-three miles per hour has about a one-in-ten chance of dying.
> At forty-two, it's a coin flip.
> At fifty-eight, almost nobody survives.
> Every ten miles per hour is the difference between a broken leg and a funeral.
> [beat]
> So if you walk at night: cross where there's light, wear something bright, and assume the driver hasn't seen you — because, statistically, they haven't.
> And if you're the one who sees it happen: call 911, don't move them unless they're in danger, and press hard on any bleeding you can see.

### 8. NEXT
> You just lived through two seconds. If you want to know what happens on a motorcycle — where there's no hood, no bumper, and nothing between you and the road — that's the next video.

## Short (≈ 55 s)

> You step off the curb. Six lanes. You've done this a hundred times.
> [ON SCREEN: 0.0 s]
> The truck is doing forty-five. You see the lights, but your brain thinks they're far away.
> [0.1 s] Its front end hits your hips and chest — not your legs. It doesn't throw you over. It pushes you down.
> [0.5 s] It doesn't hurt. Your brain just flooded you with its own morphine.
> [1.5 s] Then the ground. Your brain hits the inside of your skull. Twice.
> Here's the number that decides everything: at twenty-three miles per hour, nine out of ten people survive. At fifty-eight, almost nobody does.
> And the driver was looking at a text — so he never saw you step off the curb.
