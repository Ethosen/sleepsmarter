# ep02 — What Dying From Fentanyl Actually Feels Like

- **Adapted from (FR)** : « Voici ce qu'on ressent en mourant par le fentanyl »
- **Target length** : ~7 min · ~1 000 words
- **US setting** : college-town apartment, Ohio, Friday 11 p.m. — a pill passed around by a friend
- **Resource banner** : SAMHSA 1-800-662-4357 · 911 · (988 en commentaire épinglé)
- **Adaptation notes** : en France le fentanyl reste un sujet « américain » ; ici c'est le sujet n°1 de
  santé publique. Cadrage obligatoire : **exposition involontaire via une fausse pilule**

## Long-form script

### 1. COLD OPEN
> [SFX: muffled party music through a wall]
> Someone hands you a little blue pill. "It's just a Percocet. It's from my cousin's prescription."
> It looks exactly like the real thing. Same color. Same letters stamped on it.
> [beat]
> It isn't.

### 2. CONTRACT
> Over the next few minutes, you're going to feel exactly what that pill does — inside your brain, inside your lungs, minute by minute.
> And by the end, you'll know about the one thing, sold in pharmacies, that could bring you back.

### 3. CONTEXT
> [ON SCREEN: ~70,000 overdose deaths in 2025]
> Last year, roughly seventy thousand Americans died of a drug overdose. Fentanyl is still the biggest single killer in that number.
> It's fifty times stronger than heroin. A dose about the size of two grains of salt can be enough to kill.
> And when the DEA tests fake pills seized on American streets, about half of them carry a potentially deadly dose.
> [beat]
> Nobody can see which half.

### 4. TIMELINE

#### [TIMER 00:00] — It looks like nothing
> You swallow it with a sip of beer. Nothing happens. You keep talking.
> In your stomach, the coating dissolves. The fentanyl slips into your blood, and your blood carries it straight to your brain.

#### [TIMER 05:00] — The warmth
> [X-RAY LAYER: brain]
> Your brain is covered in tiny locks called opioid receptors. Your body has its own keys for them — the chemicals it uses to fight pain.
> Fentanyl is a master key. It fits those locks better than anything your body makes.
> First, it hits your reward system. A wave of warmth spreads from your chest. Your arms feel heavy, like sinking into warm water. Everything is fine. Nothing worries you.
> [beat]
> That feeling is the trap.

#### [TIMER 08:00] — The nod
> Your eyelids get heavy. Your chin drops toward your chest, then jerks back up. People call it "nodding."
> Your pupils shrink to tiny black points. If someone looked into your eyes right now, they'd see the first warning sign.
> They don't. They think you're just tired.

#### [TIMER 10:00] — The part nobody tells you
> [X-RAY LAYER: brainstem]
> Deep at the base of your brain, there's a small cluster of cells that has been setting the rhythm of your breathing every second since you were born. You've never had to think about it.
> It is covered in the same opioid receptors.
> Fentanyl reaches it — and the rhythm slows. Sixteen breaths a minute. Then ten. Then six.
> [beat]
> And here's what makes it so deadly: you don't feel like you're suffocating.
> Normally, when carbon dioxide builds up in your blood, an alarm in your brainstem makes you gasp for air. Fentanyl switches that alarm off too.
> You aren't fighting for breath. You're just… falling asleep.

#### [TIMER 15:00] — The sound
> [SFX: deep, irregular snoring]
> Your friends hear you snoring. Loud, slow, gurgling.
> It sounds like deep sleep. It isn't. It's the sound of an airway that's partly blocked and a body that is barely breathing.
> Your lips start to turn blue. Then your fingertips. Your skin goes gray and clammy.
> [ON SCREEN: pinpoint pupils · slow or no breathing · blue lips · snoring/gurgling · won't wake up]

### 5. POINT OF NO RETURN
> Your blood oxygen falls below the level your brain can survive on.
> Brain cells are the hungriest cells in your body. Starved of oxygen, they begin to die after about four minutes.
> Your heart, also starving, starts beating irregularly. Then it stops.

### 6. THE LAST THING YOU FEEL
> The last thing you feel is… nothing in particular.
> Warmth. Heaviness. The music through the wall.
> There's no struggle, no moment of panic. Your brain simply stopped asking for air — and you never noticed.
> [SFX: snoring stops] [BLACK]

### 7. WHAT SAVES YOU
> [warm light — a hand holding a nasal spray]
> But rewind four minutes. Someone notices you're not waking up.
> In 2023, the FDA approved naloxone — Narcan — to be sold without a prescription. It's a nasal spray. No training needed.
> Remember the master key? Naloxone is a key that fits the same locks even better — but doesn't turn them. It pushes fentanyl off the receptors and lets your brainstem start the rhythm again.
> Within two or three minutes, you take a huge breath. You wake up.
> [ON SCREEN: 1. Call 911 · 2. Spray naloxone in one nostril · 3. Rescue breaths / roll on side · 4. Second dose after 2–3 min if no response · 5. Stay until help arrives]
> Fentanyl can outlast naloxone, so you can slip back under. That's why the person must stay watched and why 911 is non-negotiable.
> Most states have Good Samaritan laws that protect people who call for help during an overdose.
> [beat]
> And the simplest rule: if a pill didn't come from a pharmacy with your name on the label, you have no idea what's in it.

### 8. NEXT
> If you want to know what alcohol poisoning does to you — the other way people die at parties without anyone noticing — that's the next video.

## Short (≈ 55 s)

> Someone hands you a pill. "It's just a Percocet." It isn't.
> Fentanyl. Two grains of salt can kill you.
> Minute five: warmth. Your arms feel heavy. Nothing worries you.
> Minute ten: the part of your brain that runs your breathing gets switched off. Sixteen breaths a minute. Then six.
> And you don't panic — because the alarm that makes you gasp for air is switched off too.
> Your friends hear snoring. It's not snoring.
> What brings you back: Narcan. Sold without a prescription. One spray in the nose.
> So if someone hands you a pill that didn't come from a pharmacy… you already know what it might be.
